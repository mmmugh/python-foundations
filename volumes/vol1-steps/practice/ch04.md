## Extra practice: a launch console

This page is optional: one more project for unit 4, if you want it. Nothing later needs it.

The model rocket club flies only when four readings are within their limits, and at the moment somebody checks them on a clipboard. You can already choose between messages with `if`, `elif` and `else`, join tests with `and` and `or`, put overlapping tests in the right order, and catch a word typed where a number belongs. By the end of this page you will have a console that reads all four readings and says whether the rocket can go.

| Reading | Safe |
| --- | --- |
| Wind | under 30 mph |
| Temperature | from 2 to 35 °C, both ends included |
| Cloud ceiling | 2000 feet or more |
| Fuel | 95 percent or more |

Each step names the exact lines it prints. The questions your `input()` lines ask are yours to word.

### Steps

1. Ask for the wind speed in mph. It can have a decimal point, like 12.5. Print `Wind: OK` when it is under 30, and `Wind: TOO STRONG` otherwise. 30 exactly is too strong.
2. Ask for the temperature in °C. It can have a decimal point too. Print `Temperature: OK` when it is from 2 to 35, with both ends allowed, and `Temperature: OUT OF RANGE` otherwise.

> Try the ends, 2 and 35, and just past them, like 1.5 and 35.5.

3. Ask for the cloud ceiling in feet, as a whole number, and sort it into three bands: `Ceiling: CLEAR` at 5000 or more, `Ceiling: MARGINAL` from 2000 to 4999, and `Ceiling: TOO LOW` below 2000.

> The tests overlap, so the narrowest goes first: the one that the fewest ceilings pass.

4. Ask for the fuel as a whole-number percent. Print `Fuel: OK` at 95 or more, and `Fuel: LOW` below 95. If what was typed is a word, or a number below 0 or above 100, print `Fuel: BAD READING`.

> It is the month program from lesson 4.4 again: the question goes in a `try` block, the check for a number outside 0 to 100 comes first, and the `except` block prints the bad-reading line too.

5. Now all four together. Ask for the wind, the temperature, the ceiling and the fuel, in that order. Then print one line: `LAUNCH: GO` when every reading is safe, and `LAUNCH: NO GO` when any of them is not. A marginal ceiling is still safe. A bad fuel reading is not.

> A word typed for the fuel has no number to compare. The grade program in lesson 4.2 keeps its letter in a variable and prints it at the end. What could step 4 keep, instead of printing it straight away, for the verdict to ask about?

6. A plain refusal doesn't help anyone fix the problem. After `LAUNCH: NO GO`, print the first reading that failed, in the order wind, temperature, ceiling, fuel, as `HOLD: wind`, `HOLD: temperature`, `HOLD: ceiling` or `HOLD: fuel`. After `LAUNCH: GO`, print no `HOLD:` line. Copy your step 5 into this box first.

> Only the first one: an `if` and then `elif`s, in the order of the readings.

7. Finish the console. Ask for the four readings, in the same order. Print the four lines from steps 1 to 4, then the verdict, then the `HOLD:` line if there is one.

> Say it: four readings go in; which lines come out? ([How to start a problem](99-how-to-start-a-problem.html))
> Split it: ask the four questions, keeping the fuel's word in a variable; print the wind, temperature and ceiling lines; print the fuel line; print the verdict; after a NO GO, print the first reading that failed.
> Name it: a name for each reading, such as `wind` and `ceiling`. You have chosen most of them already.
> Check it: try 12.5, 20, 6000 and 97: every line is OK, and GO. Then the awkward ones: readings at their edges, like 30 for the wind, 2 for the temperature and 2000 for the ceiling; two readings failing at once, where only the first is named; and a fuel of `full`.

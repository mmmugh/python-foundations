## Extra practice: a race leaderboard

This page is optional: one more project for unit 11, if you want it. Nothing later needs it.

At the end of a 400-meter race, each runner's result is handed in as a record, a name and a time in whole seconds, like `{"name": "Priya", "seconds": 55}`. They are handed in as they come, not in the order the runners finished:

| Name | Seconds |
| --- | --- |
| Ruth | 61 |
| Priya | 55 |
| Omar | 61 |
| Nadia | 58 |

A **leaderboard** is the results fastest first. You can already sort records with a key, break a tie with a tuple, search a list one item at a time, and walk along a sorted list comparing neighbors. By the end of this page you will have the race's leaderboard program, and it will find the closest finish too.

Each step names its function exactly. That name is what the check calls, so spell it as written. Where a step gives you lines that call your function, write the function above them and keep them. Their last line prints what you started with, to show that your function left it as it was.

### Steps

1. Write `leaderboard(results)`, which gives back a new list of the records, fastest first: smallest `"seconds"` first. When two runners tie, the one whose name comes first in the alphabet goes first. The results themselves stay as they were.

```python
results = [
    {"name": "Ruth", "seconds": 61},
    {"name": "Priya", "seconds": 55},
    {"name": "Omar", "seconds": 61},
    {"name": "Nadia", "seconds": 58},
]
board = leaderboard(results)
for runner in board:
    print(runner["name"], runner["seconds"])
print(leaderboard([]))
print([runner["name"] for runner in results])
```

> Ruth and Omar tie on 61. Which kind of key did lesson 11.6 use to break a tie?

2. Write `place_of(board, name)`, which gives back the place of the runner called `name` on a board already in leaderboard order, counted the way people count: the fastest is in place 1. If nobody of that name is on the board, give back 0.

> Lesson 11.1's `find_position()` gives back a position counted from 0. What is different here, and what stays the same? Each item on the board is a whole record: which field do you compare with `name`?

3. Write `smallest_gap(times)`, which gives back the smallest difference between any two times in the list `times`, a list of whole seconds in no order. So for `[64, 52, 61, 57]` it gives back 3, the gap between 61 and 64. A list with fewer than two times gives back `None`. The list itself stays as it was.

```python
times = [64, 52, 61, 57]
print(smallest_gap(times))
print(smallest_gap([50, 41, 40]))
print(smallest_gap([58, 61, 61]))
print(smallest_gap([70]))
print(smallest_gap([]))
print(times)
```

> Sort a copy first. In a sorted list, where can the two closest times be: anywhere, or side by side? Lesson 11.5 walked along the neighbors. Once the list is sorted, which of two neighbors is bigger, so which way round do you subtract?

4. Write `closest_pair(board)`, which gives back a list of the records of the two runners whose times are closest, the faster first, from a board already in leaderboard order. If two pairs are just as close, give back the pair nearer the top of the board. A board with fewer than two runners gives back `[]`.

> On a board in order, the closest two are neighbors, as in step 3. This time, keep where the closest pair is, not only its gap: how did lesson 11.4 keep the position of the smallest so far? Which comparison, `<` or `<=`, keeps the first of two gaps that are the same?

5. Put the leaderboard together. Results are handed in one at a time: ask for a runner's name, then their time in whole seconds, and keep asking until the name is left blank. At least two runners are handed in. Then print the leaderboard, one runner a line, numbered from 1, like `1. Priya 55`. Ask for a name to look up, and print `Nadia is in place 2`, or `Zed did not run`, with the name that was typed. Last, print the two runners whose times are closest, as `Closest finish: Omar 61 and Ruth 61`, the faster first. Copy your functions into this box first.

> Say it: names and times go in, until a blank name, and then one more name; what comes out, and in what order? ([How to start a problem](99-how-to-start-a-problem.html))
> Split it: start an empty list of results; ask for names and times, adding a record for each, until the name is blank (lesson 5.6); put the results in leaderboard order, and keep that board; print a line for each runner; ask for the name to look up, and let its place decide the line; then print the closest pair.
> Name it: the functions are named for you. What would you call the board, the name to look up, and its place?
> Check it: try the four runners in the table, then Nadia: Priya, Nadia, Omar and Ruth, in that order, and Omar and Ruth for the closest finish. Then the awkward ones: a name that did not run; `priya`, with a small p, which is not on the board; two runners who tie, typed in the wrong order of the alphabet; and three runners an equal gap apart, like 50, 53 and 56.
